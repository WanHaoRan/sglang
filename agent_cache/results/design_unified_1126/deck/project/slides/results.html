This slide collects what the GPU has measured so far, setting by setting. The first row is the setting of the earlier runs: 128 users and a 160 gigabyte host pool for two hours. The current design gives a mean time to first token of 10.97 seconds there. The hold alone, that is the current design with the one change that a prefill pass no longer waits on the copy of its history from host memory, gives 9.36 seconds: 1.61 seconds less on the same requests. Everything together, the hold and the cache rules of Fetch, Retain, Preempt and Price, gives 9.18 seconds: 1.79 seconds less, with an interval from 1.59 to 2.00, about 16 percent. So the cache rules add 0.22 seconds to the hold, which is what the simulator had predicted for these users, 0.28 seconds. Where they act is clear from the run: nearly all of it comes once the SSD fills and its cleaner starts deleting, in the last 22 minutes, where the whole design is 1.16 seconds faster than the hold alone. There the share of prompt tokens recomputed although they had been cached falls from 1.27 to 0.32 percent, because pages in use are kept young and finished sessions' own pages go first. Before that, the rules read somewhat more from the SSD and cost about 0.16 seconds in minutes 50 to 70. Why the hold works: without it, a prefill batch costs the running turns the full time of its copy; with it, only its own compute, and the GPU runs 4.8 percent more decode steps a second. At saturation the queue's accounting turns that into fifteen percent of the wait. The second row runs the same users with the SSD cleaner deleting from minute 50 instead of minute 88, as on a store half the size: there everything together is 2.49 seconds faster, 9.07 against 11.56 seconds, because the Retain rules act for an hour instead of twenty minutes. Once the cleaner deletes, the share of prompt tokens recomputed although cached stays between 0.2 and 0.5 percent, against 1.4 to 1.8 percent in the current design. The third row has a tight host pool, 112 gigabytes: there the gain is largest, 3.39 seconds, 26 percent, and the shape of the distribution changes. The current design served 43 percent of the history straight from the GPU, so half of its requests got their first token within a third of a second, while readers waited behind them, 13 percent of all requests for more than 30 seconds. The unified design serves the readers sooner: the 90th percentile falls from 63 to 37 seconds and the 99th from 113 to 78, while the median rises from 0.26 to 3 seconds, with 5 percent more throughput. On the mean, the paper's metric, it is a clear gain; a reader of medians would call it a regression, and the slide says so. The last row is the knee, 64 users, where the GPU pool fills only now and then: there everything is 0.14 seconds faster, 0.54 against 0.68 seconds, 21 percent, as the simulator had said, and the 99th percentile falls from 10.6 to 7.3 seconds. So the whole design is faster in all four settings, by 14 to 26 percent. The cache rules alone, without the hold, are 0.38 seconds faster than the current design in the first setting, and faster in every part of the run; on top of the hold they add 0.22 seconds, because the hold already takes part of what they would gain, so the two parts together give a little less than their sum. In the second setting, where the cleaner deletes for an hour, the cache rules alone are worth 0.99 seconds, the simulator's 0.93, all of it from minute 50 when the cleaner starts; the hold adds 1.45 seconds to them. So the Retain rules on the SSD are worth about a second when the cleaner deletes for an hour, and the hold is worth about one and a half seconds at saturation in both settings. One more arm ran in the first setting with the price of host room fixed at the old engine value instead of measured: 9.19 seconds against 9.18, a tie. The measured price stages reads earlier, which wastes SSD bandwidth but not time once the GPU is saturated. A last arm added the admission pass-over, which lets a later turn that fits take the GPU room a refused large turn cannot use: 9.10 seconds, a tie with everything else on. It moves the distribution instead, the median from 7.2 to 6.1 seconds and the 99th percentile from 50 to 55 seconds, as letting short jobs go first does.
