=== 1 cover
KV cache tiering for agent sessions · problem formulation
Pricing the room
When to fetch and what to evict across GPU memory, host memory and SSD, when the agent knows only a distribution of when each session returns
Haoran Wan · 6 November 2026
--- notes 1 cover
This deck states the problem behind the uncertain-hint results. N agent sessions, each with a context length and a distribution for when it returns, share GPU memory, host memory, one SSD and one GPU. Pricing host memory turns the problem into one decision per session: the expected-cost policy. The deck then asks whether those rules are the right way to solve the problem, what can be proved and what a replay of real reads shows, and ends with what a better model would add, what is still open and the next steps.

--- notes 2 setting
The setting first. Each session alternates between a turn on the GPU and a gap while its agent runs a tool. The agent tells the engine what it is waiting on, as a distribution of how long that gap lasts. During the gap the engine makes two decisions: where the session’s KV history waits, and when to read it back so that it is there when the turn arrives. Between turns, the history can stay in GPU memory, in host memory, or only on SSD. Host memory mirrors the GPU under write-through, so the room for idle histories is the host pool minus the GPU pool. Reading a history of L tokens back from SSD takes b·L/β seconds.

At 44 users and 70 GB, the p50 hint used as if it were exact ties with sending no hint at all: 1.51 s against 1.47 s. Exact hints under the same just-in-time routine reach 0.50 s. The cost sits in host memory. Early starts on two-moded keys, and gaps that run past the p90, pin reads long before admission. More turns read from SSD as well: 2.7 times as many as with exact hints. Eviction acting on the p50 drops histories that come back early, and pins push histories out too; these runs do not separate the two.

--- notes 10 b-model
That was step one. Five real turns, and one pattern. Reading late makes the turn wait for its read. === 11 turncost
The model
A turn pays for queueing, stalling and prefill
[img:TTFT = W + S + (Δ + U) / ρ]
Δ: the turn’s new tokens, prefilled in any case. ρ: the GPU’s prefill rate, about 9K tokens/s.
[img:W]
queue wait
From arrival A to admission D = A + W, while the GPU serves other turns.
[img:S = (ℓ − D)+]
stall
The history’s read lands at ℓ, after the turn is due at D.
[img:U]
recomputed history
Tokens lost (evicted, or read revoked or cut) and not read back. Reading costs under half as much, on another device: a failure, not an option.
Sessions are coupled through W: one session’s recomputation lengthens the wait of every turn queued behind it.
--- notes 11 turncost
Time to first token has three parts. Queue wait is set by everyone’s prefill work. Stall is the part the fetch policy controls directly. Recomputation happens only when history was lost, because reading it back costs under half as much and uses the SSD rather than the GPU. A recomputed history costs its own turn prefill time and lengthens the wait of every turn queued behind it.
=== 12 problem

The model
The engine minimizes total TTFT under shared limits
[img:Minimize over policies π the expected total TTFT over turns, subject to: the sum over sessions of h_i(t) + p_i(t) ≤ R at every t (host room); SSD reads + writes ≤ β (one SSD); one GPU, first come first served (gives the queue wait W)]

p: tokens pinned by a read staged for it

β: SSD bandwidth, 2.11 GB/s
Each tick the policy π sees L, k, a, h and p for every session, the queue and the hints, and decides:
Retain
which idle histories stay in the room
Fetch
for each history not in the room: start its read now, or wait for the arrival
Pins

--- notes 12 problem
Written as an optimization: minimize total time to first token, subject to the host room at every instant, one SSD whose bandwidth reads and writes share, and one GPU whose first-come-first-served queue produces the wait. The policy makes three decisions each tick: which idle histories to keep, when to start each read, and whether a staged read can be reclaimed. The sessions interact only through the room, the SSD and the queue.

=== 14 price
The policy
Pricing host memory lets each session decide alone
[img:E[sum over turns of TTFT] + λ E[∫ Σi (h_i + p_i) dt] − λRT = Σi J_i(λ) − λRT + const, where J_i(λ) = E[S_i] + E[U_i]/ρ + λ E[∫ (h_i + p_i) dt]]
− λRT: the room itself is free
λRT is the rent on the whole room for the whole run, R tokens for T seconds. Subtracting it leaves a charge only on use beyond the room. It is the same for every policy, so no decision depends on it.
const: queue waits and new tokens’ prefill.
Each session decides alone
It weighs its own stall against its own rent and minimizes its own J. The queue and the SSD’s speed are taken as given.
The room holds on average
The rent keeps demand near R on average, not at every instant: Whittle’s move for restless bandits. Rankings handle the instants.
The rent is learned
λ, in seconds of TTFT per token-second, is the room’s shadow price: zero while room sits idle, high when reads are refused room.
--- notes 14 price
The answer is a rent. Instead of deciding centrally who gets the room, the engine posts a price for it, and each session compares that price with what the room saves it. The room constraint couples all sessions. Replacing it with a price, λ seconds of TTFT per token-second of host memory, turns the problem into a sum of per-session problems. This is the standard Lagrangian move, as in Whittle’s relaxation of restless bandits: the room is respected on average, and the price is raised or lowered until it is. With the room idle the price is zero; with reads being refused room, it is high. The last term, λRT, is the rent on the whole room for the whole run: R tokens for T seconds, where T is the run’s length. The room itself is free, so its rent is subtracted, and what the price really charges is use beyond the room. That term is the same whatever the sessions do, so it changes no decision. It matters in two places: rule 4 raises the price while demand is above R and lowers it while demand is below, and the floor on a later slide subtracts it. Const collects the queue waits and the new tokens’ prefill, which are fixed once the queue is taken as given. The price is learned, not configured.

--- notes 15 onegap
Before the rules one by one, here is where each of them acts. This is one gap from start to finish, and each rule answers one question. Rule 1: when does the read start? Rule 2: a turn has arrived and needs room, so which early read gives way? Rule 3: the room is full, so which idle history leaves? Rule 4: what is the rent right now? In order: the turn ends and the key is known; the history is mirrored to host memory and SSD. While room is short, rule 3 keeps it in the host room only as long as it ranks high enough; otherwise it lives on SSD alone. Rule 1 picks the start time σ* when the gap begins and re-solves only when an input changes, the price, the predicted wait or the read rate, because while the session just stays idle the best start does not move. The read lands and waits as a staged read, pinned until admission; rule 2 may hand its room to a turn that has already arrived, and rule 1 then plans a re-read. If the turn arrives first, its arrival starts the read. Under all of it, rule 4 moves the price every few seconds. The next slides take the rules in this order.

Rule 2 decides who gives way when an arrived turn needs room and the room is full of staged reads. Without pins, as in original HiCache, LRU throws staged reads away before their turns, and half of what it read from SSD was lost. Soft pins take the room from the staged read least likely to be needed soon. Rule 3 decides which idle history leaves the room when it is full; the history stays on SSD. Each history is scored by the chance it returns within a horizon H, times the cost per token to bring it back: 47 microseconds from SSD, 110 for pages the SSD lacks. The lowest score goes first. Compared with the rent, the session’s own keep test comes out of the same derivative as rule 1. Holding a history a moment longer costs the rent on its tokens. Rule 4 sets the price. Every few seconds the engine compares the demand for room with the room: tokens that wanted room and were refused push the price up, room that sat idle pulls it down. This is dual ascent on the room constraint, as in network congestion pricing. The update is multiplicative because the price spans orders of magnitude: near zero at 160 GB, and in the replay of the 70 GB run the best fixed price is five times ten to the minus five for a 90K-token room, moving five-fold between a 70K and a 110K room. --- notes 24 proof
Can we prove the rules are the right way to solve the problem? Three statements can be proved, each weaker than the last. First, for one session with the rent given, rule 1 is exactly that session’s optimum. Take the derivative of its cost in the start time: waiting adds stall if the turn comes within one read time and saves rent otherwise, and the two balance where the chance of arriving within one read time equals q. That is the newsvendor’s critical ratio. The same derivative, taken for a history that is already in the room, gives rule 3 its test: keep it while its return rate times its read time is at least q. So fetching and keeping share one threshold, and rule 3 needs no separate horizon. For keys with two modes the threshold is not enough and the session’s optimum is a search over idle time: on a made-up two-moded key the best plan keeps the history through the short mode and then lets it go. Second, for all sessions together there is a floor. For any rent, add up the per-session optima and subtract the rent on the whole room, λRT: no policy that respects the room and sees the same hints can cost less. The reason takes two lines. A policy that respects the room holds at most R tokens for T seconds, so the rent it pays is at most λRT, and its cost with rent, less λRT, is at most its real cost. And each session’s optimum with rent is at most what that policy gives it. That is weak duality. At the best rent the sessions ask, on average, for exactly the room: the rent paid and the rent subtracted cancel, and the floor is the cost of plans that fit the room on average, not at every instant. It does not say the rules are optimal; it gives a number to measure them against. A policy with exact arrival times is outside it, because it knows more. Third, when sessions interact only through the room and each is small next to it, ranking by the same scores closes the gap to that floor as the number of sessions grows. That is the theory of index policies for restless bandits. It has not been written out for this model: the proof would follow Brown and Smith’s, where breaking ties and mixing two plans at the margin matter. What cannot be proved is that the rankings are optimal at a given size: the coupled problem is PSPACE-hard in general, and with exact hints and histories of unequal size it is still NP-hard. Related results back the single rules: ranking by return rate is the hazard-rate order that bounds non-anticipative caching (Panigrahy and co-authors), and the pre-warm and keep-alive windows of serverless platforms are the same two decisions with fixed percentiles in place of a rent.

The model prices one resource, the host room, and the two settings show where that is enough and where it is not. Where room is short, the p50 hint’s errors are paid in pinned memory and refused reads, and it ties with no hint. Where room is ample, landing early is free and the p50 ties with exact hints. What binds there is the GPU: recomputing lost history delays every queued turn, so a recomputed token costs about one plus the queue length times its prefill time. --- notes 31 better
Would a better model, or a different goal, do better? Three changes, each tied to a measurement. First, decide more. The formulation owns the host-only room, 44K of the 712K tokens of memory at 70 GB, and leaves GPU eviction as HiCache does it. But most idle histories wait in GPU memory, so that is where eviction decides whether a turn has to read from SSD. The replay says timing covers only 36 percent of the way to exact hints and the rest lies in which histories were evicted, so one score for every history in memory, GPU and host, is the change most likely to pay. Second, price more. Today only host memory has a price. A recomputed token costs its own prefill and also delays every turn queued behind it, about one plus the queue length times as much, and the SSD is 87 percent busy at 128 users. With those prices a turn that finds no room should wait for it instead of recomputing, and an early read has to earn more. In the replay, charging recomputation ten times its prefill leaves the rules level with reading at arrival. Third, a different goal. Mean TTFT at one load sits on a steep knee: at 44 users it falls 3.4-fold with p50 hints, while the load carried at a mean TTFT of 5 seconds rises by about 6 percent, and 11 percent with exact hints, read roughly from swings within one run. Sessions per GPU under a TTFT target is the number an operator buys. Under a target, a stall shorter than the slack costs nothing, and what matters is never losing the history of a turn that is already queued and never recomputing behind a queue. Two more levers sit outside the model: the information in the hint itself, where a session’s own history sharpens the tail, and the host mirror, which takes 668K of the 712K host tokens at 70 GB.

What could be better · open questions
Three parts of the formulation are still open
A floor for a small room
Pricing the average room gives a loose floor when the room holds two or three histories and fills in bursts. A price that varies in time, or a model of refused reads as blocking, should give a tighter one.
Two-moded keys
pip sends p10 0.85 s and p50 22 s. A threshold can fire in the short mode and pin the read through the long one. On a toy key like it, at λ = 5 × 10⁻⁶, the session’s exact plan keeps the history through the short mode, then lets it go.
The hint’s form
For timing reads, p10/p50/p90, a 24-bin histogram and every sample land within 3 % of each other in the replay. The histogram’s case now rests on eviction, an “ended” bin and merging keys.
--- notes 32 open
Three things the formulation does not settle. First, a floor that is tight in a small room. The floor from pricing the room on average is loose when the room holds two or three histories and fills in bursts, which is the 70 GB setting. A price that varies in time, or a model that treats refused reads as blocking, should give a tighter one; neither is worked out. Second, two-moded keys. The closed-form threshold assumes the chance of arriving soon only grows with idle time, and two-moded keys break that. Solving the session’s problem exactly on a made-up pip-like key, at a low price, keeps the history through the short mode and then lets it go, which is 4 to 8 percent cheaper than the best single start time; at the 70 GB price it simply waits for the arrival. So a staged read should be released once its mode has passed, not held until admission. Third, how the distribution is encoded on the wire. In the replay, three quantiles time reads as well as a histogram or every sample, so the histogram’s case rests on eviction, the explicit end and merging keys.

Where each result is used. Whittle’s relaxation is the move of pricing the room so that sessions separate. Weber and Weiss, and Brown and Smith, give the conditions under which ranking by the priced scores is near-optimal with many sessions; Adelman and Mersereau compare the relaxations of problems coupled only through shared resources.
