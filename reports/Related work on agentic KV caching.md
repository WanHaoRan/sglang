# Prior art owns each rule, not their coupling

None of the design's five mechanisms is new on its own: each has a classic ancestor, and the forecasts, the fetch threshold and the price also have 2025–2026 LLM-serving relatives that reviewers will cite. **Continuum** already keys per-tool gap distributions by the command's first word and **CacheWise** already conditions them on elapsed time; the critical-ratio start time is the operations-research earliness–tardiness newsvendor, which **Hermes** already applies with a fixed threshold to KV prewarming; "keep while the hazard exceeds the memory multiplier" is the rule of **Ferragut et al.** and **Narayana & Kash**; the max-over-horizons index is the **Gittins index**; preemption follows **Cao et al.**'s 1995 "do no harm"; and an online-learned shadow price on KV memory already drives request routing (**arXiv 2607.03948**). What none of them has is the coupling: one host-memory price, learned by multiplicative dual ascent on a demand that counts refused and preempted reads, sets a threshold q = λL/(1+λL) that rises with prefix size and is shared by fetch and retention, while eviction and preemption are scored from the same per-session costs — all for reads that take longer for longer prefixes and hold host memory while in flight. Each classical result solves a limit of this problem (unit sizes, instantaneous fetches, free re-insertion or a fixed price), so the contribution lies in exactly the terms those limits drop, plus a weak-duality lower bound that no LLM-serving paper reports. Two claims about the setting also survive: no paper passes a gap distribution from the agent framework to the engine, and no tool-aware system times SSD-to-host reads during tool gaps. The paper therefore cannot claim the first newsvendor fetch rule, the first learned KV-memory price, the first per-tool distributions or the first idle conditioning, and it must answer three published counter-results: history-based forecasts fail late in long, environment-bound calls; a full conditional-probability eviction score lost to a median threshold; and speculative prefetch rarely pays on a contended link.

## Continuum, CacheWise and IAC already own per-tool, idle-conditioned forecasts

The forecasting half of the design is the most crowded. **Continuum** (arXiv 2511.02230, v7 September 2026) keeps an empirical CDF of past durations for each tool. The engine measures each duration as the gap between one request's finish and the program's next arrival. On SWE-Bench, Continuum names the tool by "the first word" inside the bash block, which is the design's `bash/pytest` key ([Continuum v7](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.02230v7)). The per-tool records and the first-word key date to v1 of **4 November 2025** ([Continuum v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.02230v1)); the cost–benefit TTL arrived by v4 in May 2026 ([Continuum v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.02230v4)). A tool with at most **K = 100** samples falls back to global records, and with at most 100 global samples Continuum uses a default TTL derived from an Exp(1) duration ([Continuum v7](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.02230v7)), much like the design's fallback from key to tool to all calls.

Continuum differs from the design in three ways. The client sends only a `program_id`, and the engine parses tool calls itself. The CDF is unconditional. And the TTL is computed once, when the request finishes, after which the scheduler only compares it with the clock ([Continuum v7](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.02230v7)). The released code implements neither of the paper's equations: at HEAD it pins for a fixed **2 s**, and only when the tool's running-mean duration is at most 2 s ([Continuum code](https://github.com/Hanchenli/vllm-continuum/blob/316a58794a6ff86b216e579b74fd56ed0c5a911f/vllm/v1/core/estimate_with_func.py)). A Continuum baseline must therefore state which variant it ran.

**CacheWise** (arXiv 2606.16824, June 2026) is the precedent for conditioning. It ranks idle KV blocks by E[τᵢ(t) | τᵢ(t) − Tᵢ > t − Tᵢ], "the expected remaining time for the tool call to complete". The estimate comes from per-tool duration distributions or, where tool names are too coarse, from distributions over TF-IDF/KMeans clusters of the tool arguments. CacheWise rebuilds its eviction heap every **3 engine iterations**, so the conditioning time stays current ([CacheWise v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.16824v1)), and its released predictor computes exactly E[T − t | T > t] ([CacheWise code](https://github.com/cachewise-project/cachewise-coding-traces/blob/181c435a090d328d00bbbee4c8eeb27d32f3abd2/tool_duration_prediction/infer.py)). Its trace table is the best published case for keys below the tool level. Run through bash, `python -c` calls have a median of **0.1 s**, `mypy` calls 10 s with a p99 of 182 s, and `docker compose` calls **22 s**; its granularity study found 100 argument clusters best, with up to 19% better session completion time ([CacheWise v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.16824v1)).

CacheWise differs from the design in where the forecast comes from: it is trained offline on 80% of sessions, and the engine parses the tool metadata. It also uses little of the forecast — only the conditional mean, with no size, read time or price. And its released predictor truncates the survival curve at the largest observed duration. By our reading of the code, a call that has outlived every recorded duration is therefore scored as returning immediately, the reverse of the right ranking under heavy tails.

Distributions per request category predate tool keys. Alibaba's ATC '25 trace study fits exponential reuse-time distributions per request category and time window, and it evicts by the fitted reuse probability evaluated at the time since a block's last access ([KVCache Cache in the Wild](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.02634)). **Intent-Aware Caching** (IAC; arXiv 2611.09715, NeurIPS 2026) fits log-normal inter-turn delays per (intent, turn) and uses their median as a reuse window ([IAC v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.09715v1)). The GitHub Copilot trace study turns LightGBM quantile regressors into a survival curve of idle time and conditions it as S(t)/S(t₀), "requiring no additional efforts"; it reaches an ROC-AUC of 0.73 for idle periods over 60 s ([Agentic Coding in the Wild](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.00101)). The remaining agentic systems use point estimates. TokenCake blends an EWMA of past durations with a user-supplied estimate ([TokenCake](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.18586v5)), InferCept estimates a call's duration from the time it has already run ([InferCept](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01869v2)), and SYMPHONY's advisory requests carry an `expected_arrival` field ([SYMPHONY](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.16434v1)).

The interface is the one forecasting element no paper has: **no verified paper passes a gap distribution from the agent framework to the engine.** Today's hints carry one of five things: identity (Continuum's and MORI's `program_id`), structure (KVFlow's steps-to-execution, KVTether's lifecycle primitives), point timing (SYMPHONY, TokenCake), session completion (the three signals proposed in "The Storage View of KV Cache") or live progress (Ask the Tool) ([KVFlow](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.07400v1); [KVTether](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.39819); [Storage View](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.10178)). The HEAR protocol reserves room for "explicitly marked predictions" but leaves their format open, and its own retention score is a fixed decay, p·e^(−idle/400) ([HEAR](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.06597)). Applications have told systems about the future for decades. TIP let programs disclose upcoming file accesses in 1995 ([TIP](https://www.pdl.cmu.edu/PDL-FTP/TIP/SOSP15.pdf)), and TOP let phones release a 3G radio when an application predicted a long idle period ([TOP](https://doi.org/10.1109/icnp.2010.5762777)). But every such channel carries a sequence, a point or a flag. None of these papers makes the design's case for sending a distribution: that a point estimate silently fixes every read's stall risk without knowing the read's size or the memory price. The end-of-session entry, by contrast, has precedents in Continuum's release on program termination and in the Storage View paper's completion signal.

The paper most likely to be raised against the forecasts is **Ask the Tool, Don't Guess** (arXiv 2609.18849, September 2026), whose last author also co-wrote Continuum. It argues that "no estimate fixed before a call starts can know its duration, and such estimates may not even rank the calls", because a long call's duration is set by machine load, neighbours in the sandbox or remote APIs. Its evidence is strong ([Ask the Tool](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.18849v1)). Recorded commands ran an order of magnitude faster when replayed on an idle sandbox. **Fewer than 3% of calls hold almost two thirds of tool time.** Only a handful of tool identities reach Continuum's threshold of about 100 samples. And at 90% of a call, its progress stream's median error was **under 10%, against 80%** for the best history predictor; the predictors compared included a 3Sigma-style estimator conditioned on elapsed time, which is the design's own conditioning. The same paper concedes that sorting calls before they start sends "most tool time" down the right path, with precision of 96–98% and recall of 58–83%, and its accuracy metric is the error of a point estimate.

The defensible reply accepts that evidence. Late in a long call the conditional distribution is wide and F(x|t) is small, so the fetch rule declines to read early and degrades to an on-demand read. Where progress events exist, they can become one more conditioning input. TraceLab's Claude Code data show the same skew: calls longer than a minute are **4.9% of calls but 92% of tool time** ([TraceLab](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.30560)). The paper should therefore show calibration on the short calls that dominate the volume, and graceful degradation on the long tail.

Credit is due to Continuum for per-tool CDFs, first-word keys and pooled fallback; to CacheWise for elapsed-time conditioning and refinement by tool arguments; and to IAC, Alibaba and the Copilot study for per-category and survival-curve forecasts. That leaves a narrower forecasting claim about the division of labor. The framework, which knows the tool and its command, sends per-key gap distributions. The engine, which alone knows the prefix size and the price, applies the full conditional distribution — not a mean, median or quantile — to time reads and to keep, evict and preempt in host memory. The four closest papers, each read in full, contain the following.

## The fetch rule is a 1990 newsvendor with a new, size-dependent price

The fetch rule's algebra is classical. Choosing a start time that trades one second of stall per second of lateness against λL of memory cost per second of earliness is the earliness–tardiness newsvendor. Its optimum sets the probability of finishing late to the critical ratio h/(h + b); with holding cost h = λL and lateness cost b = 1, that ratio is q = λL/(1 + λL). **Weiss (1990)** set operating-room start times the same way, showing that they "depend upon a single critical number that is easily determined from the relevant costs" ([Weiss 1990](https://doi.org/10.1080/07408179008964166)). Production planning sets planned lead times with what Ould-Louly and Dolgui titled a "generalized newsboy model" ([Ould-Louly & Dolgui 2002](https://doi.org/10.1080/00207540210158825); [Yano 1987](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.33.1.95)).

In those models a supply or processing time is random against a fixed due date, costs are constant per unit, and plans are static. The design reverses the roles, with a deterministic read time and a random arrival. It also conditions on elapsed idle time and scales the holding cost with the read's size at a learned price. ArXiv searches for "newsvendor cache", "newsvendor prefetch" and "critical ratio" cache return nothing ([search: newsvendor cache](https://arxiv.org/search/?query=newsvendor+cache&searchtype=all); [search: critical ratio cache](https://arxiv.org/search/?query=%22critical+ratio%22+cache&searchtype=all&abstracts=show&order=-announced_date_first&size=50)), yet two LLM-serving papers use the same structure under other names.

**Hermes** (arXiv 2506.14851; ACM TACO, June 2026) is the closest LLM precedent for the rule's form. It prewarms backends, including "preloading the desired KV cache or LoRA". It starts each prewarm at the moment the probability that the prewarm is effective, p_e = p_s · P(t_c > t_s + t_p), equals a knob K whose default is **0.5** ([Hermes](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.14851v1)). With p_s = 1, this is the design's trigger with q fixed at 1 − K for every size and load. The differences are that K is a service-level knob rather than a value derived from costs, that it ignores size and memory price, and that the prewarm precedes a downstream call in an application DAG rather than an SSD read of an idle agent's prefix cache. The paper also calls a lower K "more aggressive", which does not follow cleanly from its Eq. (3), so the rule should be cited verbatim without characterizing its direction.

**Cheng et al.** (arXiv 2607.16892, July 2026) use the critical ratio for a different decision: the per-class KV reservation r* = L_in + F⁻¹(ρ/(ρ + 1)). They call this "the classical newsvendor critical ratio" and attribute it to operations research ([Cheng et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.16892v1)). The algebra matches the design's with ρ = 1/(λL), but the paper differs in four ways. The decision is a quantity rather than a start time. The operator sets ρ (default 10). There is no read time or conditioning. And the shared-memory constraint never enters the fractile at their operating point. Had that constraint bound, its multiplier would have added to the overage cost and produced a price-adjusted fractile — exactly the coupling the design's λ makes explicit.

Outside LLM serving, the fixed-quantile version of the rule is common and the priced version is not. RobustScaler (ICDE 2022) creates each serverless instance at the α-quantile of arrival time minus startup time, a chance constraint with α fixed for every query ([RobustScaler](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.07197)). Azure's hybrid-histogram policy prewarms at the 5th percentile of each application's idle-time histogram, a cut-off chosen by sensitivity study ([Shahrad et al.](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/atc20-shahrad.pdf)).

Two theory papers derive the threshold but assume the load is instantaneous. Narayana and Kash (UAI 2023) start the prewarm where the hazard of the next arrival reaches the ratio of holding cost to cold-start cost, and Ferragut, Carrasco and Paganini (Allerton 2024) prefetch an item once its hazard reaches the memory multiplier θ ([Narayana & Kash](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.03521); [Ferragut et al. 2024](https://aferragu.github.io/assets/pdf/allerton24.pdf)). Both are the design's zero-read-time limit: for short reads F(r|t) ≈ μ(t)·r, and the fetch rule's start time coincides with their prewarm boundary. Jiang and Kleinrock (1998) derived a prefetch threshold on access probability "based on system load, capacity, and the cost of time and system resources" ([Jiang & Kleinrock](https://doi.org/10.1109/49.669044)). This is the closest classic precedent for a probability threshold that depends on cost and load, though it decides which items to fetch rather than when.

LLM serving times its reads with point estimates. Ask the Tool refreshes a context "one lead time before the predicted return" and gives no value or selection rule for the lead time ([Ask the Tool](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.18849v1)). TokenCake uploads offloaded KV as the EWMA-predicted completion approaches ([TokenCake](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.18586v5)). **TempoKV** (arXiv 2609.35065) commits SSD staging when the estimated time to use falls to the time to ready, cutting protected fast-tier byte-time by **63–91%** against immediate staging, but only for requests already in the runtime's queue ([TempoKV](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35065)). CachedAttention, LMCache and PCR likewise prefetch only for queued requests ([CachedAttention](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.19708); [LMCache](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.09665)). KVFlow and CacheScout prefetch shared agent prompts from CPU to GPU, not per-session prefix caches from SSD ([CacheScout](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.14624v1)).

The classic integrated-prefetching rules mark the design's limits from the other side. Cao et al.'s "First Opportunity" is its λ → 0 limit, and TIP's prefetch horizon is its limit under exact forecasts ([Cao et al. 1995](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~karlin/papers/sigmetrics.pdf); [TIP](https://www.pdl.cmu.edu/PDL-FTP/TIP/SOSP15.pdf)). Picocenter is the closest structural precedent. It swaps mostly idle cloud applications to storage and prefetches their working set when a request revives them ([Picocenter](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~dml/papers/picocenter_eurosys16.pdf)). Picocenter swaps in on arrival; the design schedules the swap-in before arrival, under a memory price.

Three 2026 results make the fetch rule's price necessary rather than decorative. A GPU/DRAM/SSD placement simulator found prefetch "uniformly negative" on a link whose bandwidth is charged; even an oracle prefetcher was slower than no prefetch in over half its cells, because "a speculative fetch delays the demand fetch" ([Where Should the KV Cache Live?](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.16215)). EfficientAgent found that offloading pays only when the host tier holds the agent pool's reuse working set; a tier sized to that working set cut recomputed tokens by 93% and end-to-end time by 39% ([EfficientAgent](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33762)). FlatCake reports that "holding host staging buffers for in-flight prefixes causes contention under load that aborts prefetches" ([FlatCake](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.38355)). The design's price, and the storage bandwidth its reads share with writes, are built to suppress early reads in exactly these regimes, and the evaluation must show that they do.

Weiss, the planned-lead-time literature, RobustScaler, Hermes and Cheng each deserve credit. Once they have it, the fetch claim comes down to four points. First, the threshold is derived from priced earliness and tardiness rather than set as a knob, so q rises with prefix size and with a learned price. Second, the risk is measured over the read's own duration, which grows with the prefix, net of the predicted queueing delay (x = r − Ŵ), so a larger read accepts more risk yet still starts earlier. Third, the distribution is a per-key gap distribution conditioned on idle time. Fourth, the reads are SSD-to-host reads of idle agent prefix caches during tool gaps, which no tool-aware paper performs on the strength of a forecast.

### Preemption generalizes Cao's 1995 "do no harm"

The principle behind preemption is old. The third rule of integrated prefetching and caching in Cao, Felten, Karlin and Li is "never discard block A to prefetch block B when A will be referenced before B" ([Cao et al. 1995](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~karlin/papers/sigmetrics.pdf)), and the design already cites it. TIP ejects a hinted block and re-prefetches it later when the savings elsewhere exceed "the cost of ejecting the block" ([TIP](https://www.pdl.cmu.edu/PDL-FTP/TIP/SOSP15.pdf)). That is the deterministic precursor of preempting a staged read and letting the fetch rule replan.

In LLM serving, the closest mechanisms release memory in other orders. Continuum unpins the program with the latest arrival time to break deadlock, MORI demotes the most idle programs, and TokenCake's upload budget protects critical requests ([Continuum v7](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.02230v7); [MORI](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.00866v2); [TokenCake](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.18586v5)). TempoKV takes the opposite stance and never revokes a committed staging claim ([TempoKV](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.35065)). FlatCake attributes part of Mooncake's SSD read amplification to "revoked prefetches", which shows that revocation already happens in practice with no policy behind it ([FlatCake](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.38355)).

**No paper found orders the revocation of staged reads by the probability of timely use.** The design preempts in increasing order of F_j(r_j|t), which frees memory at the lowest expected stall per token. It then replans the preempted read and counts the preemption as demand in the price update. This is a small rule that nobody has claimed, and most of its credit should go to Cao.

## Ferragut's hazard-equals-price rule and the Gittins index anticipate retention

The retention rule is prior art in caching theory. **Ferragut, Rodríguez and Paganini (SIGMETRICS 2016)** maximize hit rate over per-item timers subject to an average-occupancy constraint. They prove that, under decreasing hazard, each timer stops where the hazard equals the memory multiplier, η(T*) = p* ([Ferragut et al. 2016](https://grupo-mate.github.io/assets/pdf/sigmetrics16.pdf)). In their Allerton 2024 paper, Ferragut, Carrasco and Paganini state the general rule — "an item is stored in the local memory if and only if its current hazard rate is greater than the threshold", where the threshold is a Lagrange multiplier — and extend it to prefetching under increasing hazard ([Ferragut et al. 2024](https://aferragu.github.io/assets/pdf/allerton24.pdf)). Carrasco, Ferragut and Paganini (Stochastic Systems 2026) prove that, for renewal arrivals, ranking items by current hazard is the optimal causal policy, and that fixed thresholds enforce occupancy only on average ([Carrasco et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.00279)).

**Narayana and Kash (UAI 2023)** place serverless keep-alive windows where c_p − c_cs·λ(x | H) changes sign ([Narayana & Kash](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.03521)). The design's rule, μ(t)·r(1 + λL) ≥ λL, is that condition with holding cost c_p = λL and miss cost c_cs = r(1 + λL). With unit hit value and no size, Ferragut's multiplier p is the design's q/r.

The same first-order condition appears in ski rental with a known distribution. Cui and Dinitz's marginal identity, c(A_{t+1}) − c(A_t) = Q_t − b·p_{t+1}, compares a discrete hazard with the ratio of rent to purchase price, though the paper does not call it a hazard ([Cui & Dinitz](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.21104)). Power management has long chosen the timeout that minimizes expected cost under a learned idle-time distribution, which comes within e/(e − 1) of the offline optimum ([Augustine, Irani & Swamy](https://math.uwaterloo.ca/~cswamy/papers/dpm-journ.pdf)).

The retention index's form is also known. Scully and Harchol-Balter state the M/G/1 Gittins index for jobs of unknown size as sup_{y>x} P[S ≤ y | S > x] / E[min{S, y} − x | S > x] ([Scully & Harchol-Balter](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.10703)). Dividing the design's ν by (b/β)(1 + λL) gives exactly this index, with S the idle gap, x the idle age and y = a + H. The design's "exact forecasts give Belady" mirrors Gittins's "known sizes give SRPT".

In caching, **Bilardi and Versaci's Least Profit Rate** policy (TCS 2013) evicts the item whose best ratio of expected hits to expected occupancy, maximized over stack depths, is lowest. The paper derives this from a relaxation that views "occupancy as a cost rather than as a constraint" and composes per-item solutions ([Bilardi & Versaci](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1109.6643)). It is the discrete ancestor of both the index and the Lagrangian decoupling. Other caching papers hold related pieces. **LHD** ranks objects by hit probability per byte-second at a single horizon ([LHD](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/nsdi18/nsdi18-beckmann.pdf)), and **EVA** subtracts the cache's average hit rate per line times expected residence, an explicit price on space-time ([EVA](https://people.csail.mit.edu/sanchez/papers/2017.eva.hpca.pdf)). **Panigrahy et al.** bound every policy that cannot see the future by ranking on hazard; for byte hits, size cancels, just as the design's per-token reload cost b/β does ([Panigrahy et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.06457)). **Raven** learns next-arrival distributions in order to imitate Belady ([Raven](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10424505)).

In LLM serving, Gittins indices appear as schedulers rather than eviction indices: Hermes ranks application units by one ([Hermes](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.14851v1)). Gast et al.'s shifted empirical Gittins index, computed from samples, is a ready-made estimator for the design's per-key tables ([Gast et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.24703)).

The LLM retention rules each implement one piece. CacheWise approximates Belady with a conditional mean. IAC orders entries lexicographically by a median-age test, then continuation probability, then size, imitating a Belady-style oracle that applies a threshold and breaks ties by size ([IAC v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.09715v1)). HEAR decays a return probability with a fixed 400 s constant. Pensieve divides recomputation cost by the time since last activity ([Pensieve](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.05516)).

Continuum is the instructive contrast. Its Eq. (2), argmax_τ P(τ, f)·(T·η + Prefill-Reload(r)) − τ, charges the full TTL even when the tool returns early ([Continuum v7](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.02230v7)). By our derivation, its interior optimum therefore solves p(τ)·B = 1 with the unconditional density p. Charging the expected occupancy E[min(D, τ)] instead yields the hazard condition h(τ)·B = 1. Since p ≤ h, under single-crossing conditions Continuum releases memory no later than a hazard rule with the same benefit B would. And because its memory term cancels, each second of pinning costs exactly one second, whatever the request's size and however much memory is free. GearUp's serverless keep-alive, argmin_D [D·C_keep + C_cold·P(τ > D)], makes the same full-timeout simplification with a fixed C_keep ([GearUp](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.38196)). The 2026 revisit of the five-minute rule supports the design's short holding times: on GPU hosts with high-IOPS SSDs, the DRAM↔flash break-even "collapses from minutes to a few seconds" ([Five-Minute Rule 40 Years Later](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.03944)).

IAC's Appendix B.4 is the counter-result to answer. Its "Full-Horizon" variant replaced the binary median-age test with "the probability that the next turn arrives within the next H seconds given the current age of the entry". That is the design's F(H|t) at one fixed H, and it lost on ShareChat at every capacity ([IAC v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.09715v1)):

| Capacity (tokens) | Full-Horizon hit rate | IAC hit rate |
|---|---|---|
| 80K | 21.5% | 23.7% |
| 110K | 27.4% | 29.8% |
| 140K | 31.0% | 34.6% |
| 170K | 35.3% | 38.5% |

The authors give two reasons. First, a near-unique continuous score dominated their lexicographic order, so continuation probability and size "rarely take effect". Second, full per-(intent, turn) distributions are "noisy", whereas the median is robust.

The first reason is an artifact of lexicographic ordering that a single scalar index avoids. B.4's score also differs from the design's index in several ways: it had no weighting by size, read cost or price, no normalization by expected holding time and no max over H, and it was tested on chat traces in a single GPU tier with hit rate as the objective. The second reason is a real risk for the design. The paper should answer it with calibration plots of F(x|t) per key, an ablation against a median threshold, and shrinkage of sparse keys toward pooled entries. IAC's own released code ranks by a continuous conditional probability over a 40-second horizon rather than by its paper's Eq. (1) ([IAC code](https://github.com/hasanar1f/intent-aware-caching/blob/085e226ef8b52244ed607f86849e6ecfa10648e9/iac/policy.py)), so an IAC baseline must also say which variant it ran.

What remains novel in retention is the cost weighting, not the rule. The design charges in TTFT seconds. A reload takes longer for longer prefixes and holds host memory while in flight, and that in-flight charge produces the (1 + λL) factor. The factor favors longer prefixes at equal hazard and has no counterpart in unit-size or byte-hit models.

Costly refetch is also what justifies the max over horizons. In Carrasco et al. and Panigrahy et al., an item evicted during a lull returns at no cost, so the current hazard alone is optimal. A re-read that costs r seconds and memory makes it worth holding a session through the lull between two modes of its distribution. And retention and fetch share the threshold q even when reads take time, whereas Narayana and Kash share a threshold only when loading is instantaneous.

The latency objective has its own precedent: delayed-hits caching showed that Belady is not latency-optimal when requests queue behind fetches in flight ([Atre et al.](https://doi.org/10.1145/3387514.3405883)). The Belady and LRU limits are standard as well; LRU follows from one shared distribution with decreasing hazard by Carrasco et al.'s Corollary 1. The paper should present these limits as consistency checks rather than contributions.

## Learned memory prices exist; a host-memory price with a duality bound does not

The design's pricing machinery is Everett's generalized Lagrange multiplier and Kelly's congestion pricing ([Everett 1963](https://doi.org/10.1287/opre.11.3.399); [Kelly et al. 1998](https://doi.org/10.1057/palgrave.jors.2600523)), and caching theory applied it to cache capacity a decade ago. **Dehghan et al. (INFOCOM 2016)** tie LRU's characteristic time to the capacity constraint's Lagrange multiplier. They learn the multiplier online with α ← max{0, α + γ(B_curr − B)} and emulate LRU with timers t = 1/α ([Dehghan et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1601.06838)). That is the additive counterpart of the design's λ ← λ·e^(ηg), but with Poisson arrivals (so timers ignore age), unit sizes and hit-probability utilities, and with excess measured as occupancy above B. Ferragut's multiplier comes from KKT conditions and fluid limits rather than online learning. Carra et al. price storage exogenously at cloud rates, and Basu et al.'s d-TTL tracks a hit-rate target with no price at all ([Carra et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.04696); [Basu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04448)).

LLM serving now has an online-learned price on KV memory, which rules out a "first" claim. **arXiv 2607.03948** (Chen, Ye and Zhou, July 2026) routes requests with dual variables it calls "the shadow price of resource type u on device g at time k", including "KV-cache memory" ([Online LP routing](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.03948v1)). It updates them online by warm-started projected subgradient steps, whose gradient is the capacity share minus the resource use of candidates that clear the current price. The paper reports that "in decode-heavy scenarios with long output sequences, memory prices dominate". But it prices GPU decode memory for admitting new requests, per device and period, in units of SLO reward, with additive steps, no weak-duality bound and a simulation-only evaluation.

**Adaptive KV Retention** (arXiv 2608.30830) prices host DRAM with a mean-field congestion price per suspended-context slot, α₂(λ) = max(0, λ − λ_crit)·β₃/C₂. The price is computed from the configured offered load and turned into one break-even TTL for all suspended requests. The authors note that a deployment "would require online load estimation and periodic recalibration" ([Adaptive KV Retention](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.30830v1)). **KVLearn** (SYSTOR 2026) charges retained blocks U = γ·|b|·Δt, with γ = R̄·λ/M_S recomputed from running averages. A PI controller moves an additive admission threshold θ toward 85% pool utilization. There is no Lagrangian, and the paper's typeset score differs from the one in its Algorithm 1 ([KVLearn](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.28637)). Continuum charges pinned memory at a fixed rate, MemUsage(r)/M, justified by assuming "the waiting queue always contains enough requests", and that factor cancels from the rule it solves ([Continuum v7](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.02230v7)).

Memory systems outside LLM serving have priced memory without learning a shadow price. **VMware ESX** interprets shares per page "as a price" and charges idle pages more through a tax with a default rate of 75% ([ESX](https://www.usenix.org/legacy/event/osdi02/tech/full_papers/waldspurger/waldspurger.pdf)). **Ginseng** and **XChange** set memory and cache prices from clients' bids ([Ginseng](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/ginseng-market-driven-memory-allocation/); [XChange](https://doi.org/10.1109/hpca.2015.7056026)). **RobinHood** reclaims 1% of each backend's cache per window and redistributes it in proportion to how often each backend blocks slow requests ([RobinHood](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/osdi18-berger.pdf)). **Memshare** equalizes hit-rate gradients, the condition a shadow price enforces, without an explicit price ([Memshare](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/atc17/atc17-cidon.pdf)).

The closest systems precedent for the design's update is **IMP** (MobiSys 2012). Its prefetcher weighs benefit against energy and data costs and uses "goal-directed adaptation" to meet budgets ([IMP](https://solar.cs.ucl.ac.uk/appoptimization.github.io/publications/HFG2012)). A lecture summary describes its conversion-rate update as multiplicative ([IMP slides](https://www.sambuz.com/doc/mobile-and-ubiquitous-computing-informed-mobile-ppt-presentation-894543)); check that against the paper before citing it as such.

The weak-duality bound has theoretical ancestors but no LLM-serving instance. Bertsimas and Niño-Mora bounded restless bandits with LP relaxations and derived priority indices from their optimal duals ([Bertsimas & Niño-Mora](https://doi.org/10.1287/opre.48.1.80.12444)). Bilardi and Versaci's relaxed average-occupancy optimum plays the same role for eviction ([Bilardi & Versaci](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1109.6643)). Panigrahy et al.'s hazard-rate bound limits the hit probability of every policy that cannot see the future, but it lets items re-enter the cache for free, so it complements rather than anticipates a Lagrangian lower bound on TTFT ([Panigrahy et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.06457)).

On benchmarks, the design sides with recent ski-rental work. Cui and Dinitz measure against OPT_p, the optimal policy for the true distribution, and argue that a hindsight benchmark forces Ω(b) loss ([Cui & Dinitz](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.21104)). That is the same comparison D(λ) makes, against policies with the same forecasts. The classic e/(e − 1) power-down results and FOO's flow bounds instead compare against a clairvoyant ([Augustine, Irani & Swamy](https://math.uwaterloo.ca/~cswamy/papers/dpm-journ.pdf); [FOO](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.03709)). In LLM serving, KVLearn's only bound is an empirical oracle that admits a block if it is reused within 1,000 requests, and 2607.03948 reports none ([KVLearn](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.28637); [Online LP routing](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.03948v1)).

The defensible pricing claim is therefore specific. The price covers the host memory held by idle prefix caches and by reads in flight, measured in TTFT seconds per token-second. It is learned by multiplicative dual ascent. Its demand counts refused reads, preempted staged reads, and prefix caches evicted while their index exceeds the price; that measurement makes the dual slope observable even though occupancy can never exceed R. And it comes with a weak-duality lower bound against policies with the same forecasts. None of 2607.03948, Adaptive KV Retention or KVLearn has all of these. Dehghan et al. have online dual ascent, but it is additive and driven by occupancy rather than refused demand.

## What survives crediting is one price coupling four decisions

Systems that face the same coupling resolve it by heuristics. AppFlow (MobiCom 2026) names the tension directly — preloading "reduces I/O stalls but consumes scarce memory and is undone by reclamation" — and coordinates a preloader, a reclaimer and a process killer without a shared price ([AppFlow](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17259)). Across the five mechanisms, prior work holds every rule in a special case, and none holds the coupling. The table states, for each mechanism, what to credit, what not to claim and what remains.

The claims that something does not exist have limits the paper should state. They rest on full-text checks of the closest LLM papers, dozens of arXiv listing searches, and Crossref or publisher records for classic work. ArXiv listing search does not index full text, and conference papers without arXiv versions were not searched systematically. The field is also moving weekly — IAC appeared on 6 November 2026, GearUp on 28 October, and the Storage View paper this month — so the related-work section needs a fresh sweep before submission.

## Conclusion

Each lineage solved this problem in a limit, and the design's own list of special cases doubles as a map of who owns which limit. Zero read time belongs to Ferragut et al. and to Narayana and Kash; constant costs to Weiss; a fixed quantile to Shahrad, RobustScaler, Hermes and the Storage View paper's p90 timer; and a fixed or configured price to Continuum, Adaptive KV Retention and KVLearn. λ → 0 is Cao's "First Opportunity", exact forecasts give Belady and TIP's prefetch horizon, and one pooled distribution gives LRU. Writing the related-work section around these reductions turns crediting into evidence of generality. It also pins the contribution to the interior regime where none of the limits holds: reads that grow with the prefix, into scarce host memory, with arrivals forecast as distributions.

That interior regime is also where the published evidence is most skeptical, so the evaluation, not the derivations, carries the novelty claim. Calibration of F(x|t) per key together with a median-threshold ablation answers IAC. A fixed-q ablation separates the design from Hermes, RobustScaler and the serverless percentiles, and a fixed-price ablation separates it from Adaptive KV Retention and KVLearn. A demonstration that the rules collapse to the hazard rule as r → 0 makes the lineage explicit, and runs in which reads and writes share storage bandwidth answer the 2026 placement simulator. One flank remains open in every paper, this design included: robustness to a wrong forecast. The design acts on F(x|t) as if it were exact; its only safeguard is a heavier tail for keys whose requests keep arriving past their 90th percentile. Distributional ski-rental work already offers clamps and regret bounds for wrong forecasts ([Kim & Fan](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.29233); [Cui & Dinitz](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.21104)), but nobody has extended them to many sessions coupled by one price.
