# Agent-cache evaluations replay sleeps, not tools

Across 36 LLM-serving papers on agent-aware KV retention and prefetch, agent-program and tool-call serving, 2026 KV tiering and pricing, and multi-tier KV caches, **the standard evaluation replays recorded traces on a real engine, turns every tool call into a fixed sleep, and forces each output to its recorded length**. Classified by the results they lead with, 15 papers replay traces, 9 drive synthetic chat or QA workloads, 6 simulate, 3 resist classification, and only ThunderAgent, PBKV and Hermes run live agents or applications for every headline result; Continuum and EfficientAgent pair their replays with live SWE-bench runs. Workloads centre on SWE-bench coding agents driven by mini-swe-agent or OpenHands, usually frontier-model traces replayed on smaller open models, and since mid-2026 on real-user Claude Code and Codex traces whose human waits last minutes. The serving–agent interface has converged on an OpenAI-compatible request carrying a program or session ID plus an explicit release call; richer channels carry structure, point estimates or progress, and **no paper sends a distribution of the next gap**. Our campaigns, a closed-loop replay of the LMCache agentic traces on SGLang HiCache with recorded gaps slept and output lengths forced, are the modal design and match MORI's replay loop point for point, and no other paper in the set clearly combines a real engine, agent sessions, an SSD tier and preserved tool timing. The exposures are specific: gaps stretched ×10 or ×70 where no other paper multiplies recorded gaps, a reply key read from the replay rather than parsed by the engine, frontier-model traces served on Qwen3-30B-A3B, one trace and mostly one run per arm, no published-system baseline, and no live check. Campaign 26 already shows an engine with only session linkage reaching most of the gain, so the next evaluation has to locate where distributions beat the literature's program-ID interface, and the additions that do so are the ones reviewers of this literature will ask for anyway: a human-paced arm at ×1 from FreeInference or TraceLab, a live mini-swe-agent run whose framework emits the hints, a token-exact replay of that run, and MORI plus a named Continuum variant as baselines.

## Fifteen of 36 papers replay traces; only three run live agents throughout

The 36 papers in scope, read in their latest arXiv versions as of 17 November 2026, sort into four modes once each is classified by the results it leads with. **Trace replay on a real engine carries 15**: Continuum, CacheWise, Ask the Tool's KV study, Intent-Aware Caching (IAC, on chat), InferCept, LAMPS/MARS, Autellix, Astraea, MORI, HEAR's KV experiments, KVTether, EfficientAgent's policy comparisons, FlatCake, Mooncake and Alibaba's trace study. **Synthetic workloads on real engines carry 9**: KVFlow, Adaptive KV Retention (AKR), Parrot, SYMPHONY, TempoKV, CachedAttention, Pensieve, LMCache and Strata. **Simulation or offline accounting carries 6**: KVLearn, Marconi, the Storage View study, "Where Should the KV Cache Live?", Cheng et al.'s critical-fractile reservation and the online-LP router. **Only ThunderAgent, PBKV and Hermes run live agents or applications against the engine under test for every headline result** ([ThunderAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.13692v3); [PBKV](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.06472v1); [Hermes](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.14851v1)). The remaining three resist classification: TokenCake runs its applications live but replays recorded tool outputs, Conveyor runs real tools one request at a time, and CacheScout never says whether its multi-agent sessions are live or replayed ([TokenCake](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.18586v5); [Conveyor](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.00059v2); [CacheScout](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.14624v1)).

The strongest designs pair modes rather than pick one. Continuum's headline curves replay GPT-5 traces under Poisson program arrivals, but its Fig. 12 runs "a real SWE agent for 500 tasks in SWE-Bench-Verified" on an H100 testbed, with a job distributor releasing agents by Poisson and pass rate reported beside delay ([Continuum v7](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.02230v7)). EfficientAgent runs OpenHands live on SWE-bench Verified with 16 workers in per-task sandboxes, then replays that run's 4,427 calls in a closed loop in which a task's next call waits for the previous response plus its recorded interval, and decoding is constrained "through a vLLM logits processor, to emit the recorded output tokens" ([EfficientAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.33762v1)). AKR pairs live serving with a CPU-only "measured-cost replay" for sweeps ([AKR](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.30830v1)), the same division our campaigns draw between GPU runs and the CPU room model, which replays a measured run's reads and states that "TTFT is out of reach" ([FINDINGS.md:9-25, 148-149](../agent_cache/results/cpu_calib_c44/FINDINGS.md)). **Every replay turns tool time into a sleep that ignores load.** MORI's client "sends a request, waits for the response, sleeps for the recorded tool-call or human-wait duration, and sends the next request" ([MORI](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.00866v2)); KVTether writes "We simulate tool calls by waiting for their recorded durations, eliminating variability in tool execution time across experiments" ([KVTether](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.39819v1)); InferCept's released driver calls `time.sleep` on the recorded time and returns zero tokens ([InferCept driver](https://github.com/WukLab/InferCept/blob/main/benchmarks/final_tput_bench_real.py)); TokenCake samples durations "with a fixed seed, ensuring that all baselines receive identical tool behaviors and stall times" ([TokenCake](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.18586v5)). Several replays never say how tool time enters — Continuum's, Ask the Tool's KV study, and CacheWise beyond a figure legend reading "ground truth tool durations" — and FlatCake drops it by replaying requests in recorded order at a fixed 0.1–1.0 QPS ([FlatCake](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.38355v1)). The evidence that real tool time responds to load is strong. Ask the Tool replayed leaderboard commands on an idle sandbox and **the same command finished an order of magnitude faster**, while busy neighbours made most calls several times slower ([Ask the Tool](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.18849v1)); ThunderAgent measured environment preparation time rising with the number of parallel workflows ([ThunderAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.13692v3)). Load-coupled tool time therefore exists only where tools actually run: ThunderAgent, Hermes, Continuum's Fig. 12 and EfficientAgent's live runs.

**Wherever a replay states its output policy, it forces outputs**, from token-exact to content-free. EfficientAgent returns "all 4,427 recorded sequences, every output token as recorded". MORI generates "exactly its recorded number of output tokens", including the traced models' reasoning tokens, "regardless of the served model". KVTether sends "random prompts with recorded per-round input token counts" and enforces recorded output counts, adjusting content so each request's reusable prefix matches the trace. Mooncake terminates outputs at recorded lengths on a dummy model, and Alibaba replaces generated text with constructed tokens ([Mooncake FAST '25](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/fast25-qin.pdf); [Alibaba](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.02634v5)). IAC's released replayer sends `max_tokens=1` ([IAC replay.py](https://github.com/hasanar1f/intent-aware-caching/blob/HEAD/scripts/replay.py)), and AKR generates 1, 8 or 64 tokens. Continuum's replay, CacheWise, Ask the Tool, FlatCake and CacheScout do not say. Free generation appears only in live runs, and it brings divergence that few papers handle: PBKV observes workflows that "diverge within a few tokens" and repeats each setting ten times, Continuum reports pass rate, EfficientAgent assesses quality separately in live execution, and ThunderAgent reports steps per minute with no task success at all.

**Simulation settles policy questions while engines carry the headlines.** Mooncake's capacity curve does "not account for factors such as prefill computation time" ([Mooncake FAST '25](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/fast25-qin.pdf)); Marconi's hit rates come from a CPU trace simulator that only its artifact identifies ([Marconi artifact](https://github.com/ruipeterpan/marconi)); "When Fancy Eviction Fails" evaluates eviction in its PrefixSim simulator and schedules the AgentX sessions at a steady concurrency of 16 while keeping their within-session timing ([When Fancy Eviction Fails](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.28870v3)). The simulation-only papers supply the counter-results a prefetch design must answer, but on thin footing: "Where Should the KV Cache Live?" finds prefetch "uniformly negative" in a simulator that "runs at batch one on a single GPU" and whose calibration the paper never describes ([Where Should the KV Cache Live?](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.16215v1)), and CacheScout's finding that prefetch alone "is inert" comes from sessions whose inter-turn latency is about 0.3 s. The serverless keep-alive literature shows the accepted way to combine modes: Shahrad et al. evaluate their policy in simulation and then replay 68 applications for 8 hours on a real OpenWhisk deployment ([Serverless in the Wild](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.03423v3)).

## SWE-bench agents dominate, and real-user traces arrived in 2026

**SWE-bench is the common denominator of agent workloads.** Continuum replays 100 mini-swe-agent traces and runs 500 Verified tasks live; EfficientAgent runs OpenHands v0.56.0 CodeActAgent with Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B; ThunderAgent runs mini-SWE-agent and OpenHands on SWE-bench Lite; PBKV drives SWE-bench through AutoGen and CacheScout through AutoGen's SelectorGroupChat; Ask the Tool reads about 3,000 leaderboard trajectories plus 100 runs of its own; Marconi uses SWE-agent trajectories; and 665 of the 769 chains in our own trace are SWE-bench Verified runs of OpenHands CodeAct ([stats.json:75-78](../agent_cache/traces/lmcache_agentic_trace_262k.json.stats.json)). Beyond SWE-bench the papers spread thinly: BFCL v4 web search (Continuum), BFCLv3 and LATS (Autellix), τ²-bench (AKR), HLE through ToolOrchestra and ScienceAgentBench (ThunderAgent), HoVer through LangChain and FinanceBench through CrewAI (PBKV), TerminalBench through Codex (KVTether), GAIA (CacheScout, and our trace through Inspect AI), PEER Financial QA (KVFlow), and BrowseComp-Plus and DeepResearchBench through LangGraph, which HEAR runs live only to choose sparse-attention modes ([HEAR](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.06597v1)). The InferCept lineage (InferCept, LAMPS/MARS, Astraea) shares a six-type augmentation set whose "interception" times run from 9×10⁻⁵ s for math to an estimated 28.6 s of human think time for chat, so most of its gaps are not agent tool calls ([InferCept](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01869v2)). The multi-tier papers use chat or document QA and invent the very gap that decides tier placement: Pensieve samples exponential think time with a 60 s mean, Strata inserts a fixed 60 s, Marconi varies means of 5–10 s, and CachedAttention and LMCache state none ([Pensieve](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.05516v3); [Strata](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.18572v1); [CachedAttention](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.19708v3)).

**Nearly every replay runs a stronger model's traces on a smaller open model.** Continuum chose GPT-5 because "Base small models often fail to accomplish the task" and serves the traces on Llama-3.1-8B/70B and Gemma-3-12B ([Continuum v7](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.02230v7)); CacheWise replays Claude sessions on Qwen2.5-Coder-32B; MORI replays Claude and GPT-5 traces on Qwen2.5-7B, gpt-oss-120b and Qwen3-235B; KVTether replays DeepSeek-V4-Pro and production traces on GLM-4.7. Our trace was generated by MiniMax-M2.5, Claude Sonnet 4.6, Claude Opus 4.6 and DeepSeek V3.1 and is served on Qwen3-30B-A3B ([starter kit:13](../agent_cache/agent-kv-tiering-evaluation-starter-kit.md)). Only EfficientAgent replays the served model's own live run, so its trajectories, token counts and reply text are native.

**The 2026 shift is to real-user coding-agent traces, and their timing differs in kind.** CacheWise replays held-out Claude Code sessions from lab members, 94 of which are released ([CacheWise manifest](https://github.com/cachewise-project/cachewise-coding-traces/blob/HEAD/parsed_traces/manifest.json)); MORI replays the SemiAnalysis Claude Code corpus it calls AgentX, splitting 391 sessions into 790 programs and 965 subagents, plus TraceLab; FlatCake replays the same corpus (393 sessions, 98,827 requests, 21.6B input tokens); and KVTether replays two production coding agents of a cloud provider, 400 tasks each. TraceLab finds that **human thinking is 92.3% of session wall-clock**, with positive human waits at a median of 1.4 min and a p90 of 20.6 min, and tool latencies at a median of 0.3 s and a p90 of 13.6 s ([TraceLab](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.30560v2)); in AgentX, 11% of main-agent steps end in a gap of at least 60 s, and gaps across MORI's workloads reach a p99 of 519–600 s ([MORI](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.00866v2)). Our LMCache trace has no human waits at all: its 24,111 gaps have a median of 0.708 s, a p99 of 11.76 s and only 0.66% at or above 30 s ([stats.json:47-57](../agent_cache/traces/lmcache_agentic_trace_262k.json.stats.json)). The production traces behind the M365 residency study and an 11.7M-request agent platform are not released ([M365 study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.22863v1)), so the usable public releases are those below; they differ mainly in whether they separate tool time from human time and whether they let prefixes be rebuilt.

**FreeInference is the best primary candidate and TraceLab the best second.** FreeInference alone combines real users, a per-request trigger that separates tool gaps from human waits, tool names plus the shell programs in each command — exactly our hint key of tool plus first command word — and chained block IDs for rebuilding prefix reuse. Its `latency_ms` is described as "the gap until the next LLM request", and a one-day sample shows it equals the next request's start minus this request's end at the gateway, the same semantics as the `pre_gap` our client sleeps; its limits are that block IDs are local to each week and no text is released ([FreeInference](https://huggingface.co/datasets/harvardMadsys/freeinference_agentic_trace)). TraceLab adds tool wall latency with ordered bash executables and ships `session_runner`, which loops "send round i -> wait for full LLM response -> sleep tool_wait_after_ms -> send round i+1" with synthetic text but preserved prefix and append lengths, `max_tokens` and waits, for vLLM today and for SGLang through a pluggable backend ([TraceLab replay](https://github.com/uw-syfi/TraceLab/blob/main/replay/README.md)). AgentX buys direct comparability with MORI and FlatCake, but its `think_time` mixes tool and human time and it names no tools, which MORI itself turns into a test of serving "without tool information". Gap semantics must be normalized before per-key distributions are fitted, since FreeInference measures response end to next request, TraceLab tool emission to result, and Copilot batch wall-clock with timestamps that appear to mark ends. None of these real-user traces carries text, so prompts must be synthesized to the recorded reusable-prefix lengths, as KVTether does.

## A program ID plus a release call is the de facto interface

**Most recent systems ask the agent for an identity and an end, nothing more.** ThunderAgent's integration is three changes: `program_id` in the `extra_body` of each chat completion, the same ID on tool calls, and an explicit `POST /programs/release` ([ThunderAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.13692v3)). MORI uses "a standard OpenAI-compatible chat/completion API augmented with a program_id field", a distinct one per subagent, documents the same release endpoint, batches type labels onto SGLang's radix-tree nodes, and states that "MORI needs to know only which program a tool call belongs to" ([MORI](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.00866v2); [MORI repo](https://github.com/cblmemo/mori)). Continuum's paper attaches a `program_id`; its released client sends `job_id` and `is_last_step` through `extra_body` ([Continuum client](https://github.com/Hanchenli/vllm-continuum/blob/main/mini-swe-agent/src/minisweagent/models/vllm_model.py)). Autellix opens and closes programs with `start_session` and `end_session` and tags calls with session, program and thread IDs ([Autellix](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.13965v1)); PBKV's server "continuously listens for and records workflow termination messages from clients" ([PBKV](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.06472v1)); IAC's replayer passes a conversation ID and timestamp. ThunderAgent and MORI infer reasoning and acting phases from request boundaries alone — an arriving request means reasoning, a completed response means acting — and nothing parses a tool call ([ThunderAgent router](https://github.com/ThunderAgent-org/ThunderAgent/blob/HEAD/ThunderAgent/scheduler/router.py)).

**Richer channels carry structure, a point estimate or progress, never a distribution.** KVTether's in-harness framework exposes eight lifecycle primitives (append, replace, remove, fork, spawn, one_off, pin, unpin), its server-side manager hands the cache engine a call ID and context ID before forwarding each call, and integration takes "at most 216 lines of code per harness" across six harnesses ([KVTether](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.39819v1)). TokenCake extends vLLM's OpenAI endpoint with DAG registration plus `call_start` and `call_finish` events carrying time estimates; KVFlow embeds agent identity and steps-to-execution in each request; Hermes holds per-application graphs of demand distributions and branch probabilities and talks to its workers over ZeroMQ RPC; Parrot exposes Semantic Variables; and Sutradhara exposes `tag_kv_blocks` and `set_reuse_priority` ([KVFlow](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.07400v1); [Hermes](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.14851v1); [Parrot](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.19888v1); [Sutradhara](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.12967)). On timing, SYMPHONY's advisory requests carry an `expected_arrival` point, sent on average 11.3 s ahead for chat and 5.8 s for MetaGPT ([SYMPHONY](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.16434v1)); Ask the Tool wraps the harness to emit progress events and has a controller refresh, release or query a context named by an earlier response ID through one patched engine route ([Ask the Tool](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.18849v1)); and HEAR runs both ways, with the harness sending priority, a waiting guard and a session-return probability and the engine reporting residency and realized reuse ([HEAR](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.06597v1)). HEAR's retention score still decays that probability with a fixed 400 s constant. **No paper sends a distribution of the next gap.**

**Engine-side parsing is the alternative to hints, and it assumes the engine sees raw completions.** Continuum names a tool by the first word inside the bash block, CacheWise's block manager stores `tool_name` and `tool_args` "parsed by the vLLM engine itself", and Conveyor registers a parser per tool and streams partial arguments to it during decoding ([Continuum v7](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.02230v7); [CacheWise](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.16824v1); [Conveyor](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.00059v2)). A gateway that parses tool calls before the engine would break all three. CacheScout fingerprints each request from its prefix block hashes "without any framework cooperation", FlatCake places blocks by engine-observable signals with the session ID in the block key, and EfficientAgent's admission control estimates the working set from "tasks with recent requests" without saying how the engine learns task identity ([CacheScout](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.14624v1); [FlatCake](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.38355v1); [EfficientAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.33762v1)).

**End-of-session signals are release calls, and only one paper measures their worth, offline.** Release on termination — ThunderAgent's and MORI's endpoint, Continuum's `is_last_step`, Autellix's `end_session`, PBKV's termination messages — is the field's end signal. The Storage View study compares idle TTLs of 1, 5 and 30 minutes with "reclaiming state at session completion" and finds a pooled p90 TTL of 253 s too short for two production workloads, but it does so by counterfactual accounting with "no capacity limit", not on an engine ([Storage View](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.10178v1)). For interactive sessions the end is fuzzy: MORI splits Claude Code sessions "at idle periods longer than 30 minutes and at context compactions".

**Our interface sits one step beyond all of these, and our own results test that step.** The client piggybacks hints on the OpenAI request: each request carries an `rid` of the form `<tag>-c<user>-t<turn>`, and `kv_hints` carries the session, the `next_rid` that continues it, and either the exact next gap (oracle arms) or a table of per-key gap quantiles (p10, p50, p90, n), an `_end` entry where the recorded session ends, and the recorded reply's key ([replay_agentic.py:209-232](../agent_cache/scripts/replay_agentic.py); [gap_hints.py:412-421](../agent_cache/scripts/gap_hints.py)). A full table is about 3 KB and 38 entries per request ([DIST_HINT_DESIGN.md:50-52](../agent_cache/DIST_HINT_DESIGN.md)). The engine reads it once, when the turn commits, "the same as a message sent right after the reply with no delay", and our docs call the hint "three signals in one" — session linkage, the end and the gap — noting that a real system has the first, has the second only partly, and lacks the third ([REALISTIC_HINTS.md:83-94](../agent_cache/REALISTIC_HINTS.md)). In the literature's terms, campaign 26's `nofc_noend` is the program-ID interface (it "uses the hint for nothing an engine could not see itself", and the outside reviewer labels it "session id only"), `nofc` adds the release, and the hinted arms add the distribution ([DECISIONS.md:125-127, 507](../agent_cache/results/campaign26/DECISIONS.md)). The result mirrors MORI's bet. At 44 sessions and 70 GB, `nofc` (0.69 and 0.80 s) and `nofc_noend` (0.71 s) sit beside rules 1 and 3 (0.66 and 0.78 s), with just-in-time reads on exact hints at 0.51–0.54 s, and "nothing among nofc, nofc_noend, ec_r1 and rules 1 and 3 is shown in TTFT at this setting"; at 128 sessions, `nofc_noend` lands inside the range of the four hinted arms ([DECISIONS.md:575-606](../agent_cache/results/campaign26/DECISIONS.md)).

## Our replay is MORI's loop with stretched time and a borrowed reply key

**The replay loop itself is accepted practice.** Our client replays one recorded conversation per session in a closed loop: it sends a turn, streams the reply, appends it verbatim, sleeps the recorded `pre_gap` times a gap scale (capped at 1,200 s) from the end of the stream, and then sends the next turn's recorded tool results and user messages ([replay_agentic.py:7-10](../agent_cache/scripts/replay_agentic.py); [REPLAY_REDESIGN.md:157-161](../agent_cache/REPLAY_REDESIGN.md)). Each request forces the recorded length with `max_tokens`, `ignore_eos`, temperature 0 and thinking off ([replay_agentic.py:258-270](../agent_cache/scripts/replay_agentic.py)). The server is SGLang with HiCache on one H200, serving Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507 with bf16 KV, a 668,160-token GPU pool, a write-through host pool of 70–160 GB and a NIXL SSD tier whose 2.11 GB/s is shared by reads and writes ([PAPER_PLAN.md:36-46](../agent_cache/PAPER_PLAN.md)); 44 or 128 concurrent sessions run, idle ones keeping their slots, for 2 h per arm from an empty cache ([replay_agentic.py:14-16](../agent_cache/scripts/replay_agentic.py); [DECISIONS.md:4-5, 529](../agent_cache/results/campaign26/DECISIONS.md)). That matches MORI's design point for point — SGLang HiCache, a closed loop at fixed programs in flight, recorded waits as sleeps, recorded output counts, two-hour runs — and TraceLab's runner and EfficientAgent's replay use the same loop.

**Three things already set the campaigns apart.** First, **no other paper in this set clearly combines a real engine, agent sessions, an SSD tier and preserved tool timing**: FlatCake has the first three but replays at fixed QPS, TempoKV stages from flash for 12–32 document-QA requests with no sessions, KVTether, EfficientAgent and HEAR keep timing but stop at host or remote DRAM, and Continuum's lone SSD figure sits on a replay that never says how tool time enters ([TempoKV](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.35065v1); [Continuum v7](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.02230v7)). Second, our documents disclose what most replays leave out: the gap definition, the cap, output forcing, the hint's reply-key source, and prompt-length fidelity, with replay prompts averaging 1.007× the recorded ones ([stats.json:73](../agent_cache/traces/lmcache_agentic_trace_262k.json.stats.json)). Third, the comparison is statistically stricter than the field's: arms are compared on paired returning turns, a difference counts only when it beats the largest measured rerun move (0.12–1.15 s on 6,715 turns), and two independent agents re-derived 157 of 157 client-side and 1,946 of 1,946 server-side numbers from raw logs ([DECISIONS.md:382-385, 458-460, 520-525](../agent_cache/results/campaign26/DECISIONS.md)), whereas most papers here report no variance. Our prompts also carry the recorded tool results and user messages as real text, where KVTether, Mooncake, Alibaba and every real-user replay must synthesize tokens.

**The largest exposure is the gap scale.** Every campaign multiplies recorded gaps by 10 or 70 because "at real speed the host and SSD tiers are rarely reached: only ~0.35 % of real gaps reach 60 s" ([REALISTIC_HINTS.md:567](../agent_cache/REALISTIC_HINTS.md)). Our documents name the cost themselves: "x70 manufactures hint value", since 47% of ×70 returns fall at 45–60 s, where LRU evicts, mostly OpenHands' ~0.72 s polling grid times 70 ([REALISTIC_HINTS.md:564-565](../agent_cache/REALISTIC_HINTS.md)); the cap removes 49.6% of idle time at ×70 ([REPLAY_REDESIGN.md:168-175](../agent_cache/REPLAY_REDESIGN.md)); and ×10 "is a forgiving test" because queue waits of about 30 s dwarf forecast errors ([DIST_HINT_DESIGN.md:104-108](../agent_cache/DIST_HINT_DESIGN.md)). **No other paper here multiplies recorded gaps**: Mooncake and LMCache compress time, FlatCake replaces it with a fixed rate, and papers that need long idle periods either synthesize them (Pensieve's think time up to 600 s, AKR's 1,800 s approval waits) or take them from real users (MORI). The trace also holds batch agents only, so "human waits cannot be tested on this trace" ([DIST_HINT_DESIGN.md:155](../agent_cache/DIST_HINT_DESIGN.md)), although human waits dominate real use.

**The second exposure is that no framework has produced the hints and no engine has parsed a live reply.** Because the replay's replies carry no tool call, the hinted arms set the reply-key source to `hint` and take the recorded reply's key from the request — "a workaround for the replay only" ([gap_hints.py:19-22](../agent_cache/scripts/gap_hints.py)) — so "the engine has never read its own reply on a GPU" ([PAPER_PLAN.md:17](../agent_cache/PAPER_PLAN.md)). The engine's parser matches the client's keys on all 25,617 recorded replies offline ([DIST_HINT_DESIGN.md:85](../agent_cache/DIST_HINT_DESIGN.md)), but no reply generated under load has passed through it. The online table is realistic in that it starts empty each run and learns per harness and model, yet hints arrive at commit, whereas a real post-reply message arrived 82–94 ms later at the median, and piggybacking "cannot emulate updates during the wait" ([REALISTIC_HINTS.md:525-528](../agent_cache/REALISTIC_HINTS.md)). The model mismatch compounds this: replies are content-free text of the recorded length from a model that did not produce the trace, and no document measures how that changes engine timing.

**The rest of the exposure is scope.** The campaigns use one trace, one seed and one model at TP 1, mostly with one run per arm. No published system appears as a baseline, whereas MORI has become the standard one — KVTether runs it and AKR ports it — and Continuum circulates in at least three variants: the paper's per-tool estimator (reimplemented by Ask the Tool), the public fork's fixed 2 s TTL (used by AKR) and a 0.3 s TTL (CacheScout) ([AKR](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.30830v1); [CacheScout](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.14624v1)). The headline metric, mean TTFT of returning turns, has precedents in Ask the Tool's post-tool TTFT and AKR's resume TTFT, but the outside review notes it "is not what the agent gets", and the literature also reports session outcomes: Continuum's job completion time, EfficientAgent's makespan, ThunderAgent's steps per minute, MORI's output throughput and KVTether's dollars per task. The two settings change sessions and memory together, neither stresses the SSD, and the host pool has been varied only once ([DECISIONS.md:493-504](../agent_cache/results/campaign26/DECISIONS.md)). That one test is telling: **30 GB more host memory bought 0.52 s at 44 sessions, against 0.10–0.21 s for exact hints over no hints** ([DECISIONS.md:1201-1205](../agent_cache/results/campaign26/DECISIONS.md)) — the capacity-first lesson EfficientAgent also draws when raising its host tier from 5 to 20 GiB per rank cuts computed prefill by 93.1% and makespan by 38.7% ([EfficientAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.33762v1)).

## Every exposure has a published precedent to borrow

The fixes that reviewers of this literature will ask for already exist in it. The table maps each exposure to the paper that solved it and to the concrete addition for our campaigns.

| Exposure | Precedent | Addition to our campaigns |
|---|---|---|
| Gaps stretched ×10 or ×70; batch agents only | MORI replays real developers' tool and human waits unscaled; TraceLab ships a closed-loop runner | A ×1 arm on FreeInference, with TraceLab as a second population and AgentX for comparability |
| Hints never emitted by a framework or parsed from a live reply | Continuum's live mini-swe-agent run passes IDs through `extra_body`; ThunderAgent integrates in three changes | Live mini-swe-agent on SWE-bench Verified sending `kv_hints`, engine on the `reply` key source, pass rate reported |
| Frontier-model traces and content-free replies | EfficientAgent replays its own live run token-exactly | A teacher-forced replay of our own live run |
| No published-system baseline | MORI (run by KVTether, ported by AKR); Continuum in named variants | MORI on our build, a named Continuum variant, our rivals relabelled "program ID only" and "program ID plus release" |
| Sessions and memory move together; SSD unstressed; one run per arm | MORI sweeps 20–120 programs; EfficientAgent sweeps 3–80 GiB per rank and repeats runs; PBKV runs 10 times | Concurrency sweep at fixed memory, host sweep at fixed sessions, an SSD-bound point, repeats for any claim under 0.25 s |
| Load-independent tool time; idealized hints | TokenCake's duration noise; SYMPHONY's missing advisories; LAMPS's error injection | Gap jitter plus dropped, noisy and late hints |
| Returning-turn TTFT as the only headline | MORI's throughput and per-tier served fractions; FlatCake's delivered hit rate; KVTether's dollars per task | Session completion, throughput, per-tier delivered hits, recompute fraction, sessions per GPU at a TTFT target |

**The human-paced arm comes first, because it attacks the largest exposure and the open scientific question together.** Replaying FreeInference at ×1, with prompts synthesized to the recorded reusable-prefix lengths and output lengths forced, keeps our client's loop intact, derives hint keys from recorded tool names and shell programs, and separates tool gaps from human waits through the trigger field. TraceLab adds a second population and a ready runner; AgentX adds comparability with MORI and FlatCake and, lacking tool names, tests the pooled fallback. Real human waits push idle prefix caches into the host and SSD tiers without any stretch, so this arm can also create the SSD pressure that campaign 26 lacked, provided host memory stays scarce. The `hint` reply-key source remains necessary here, since the content is synthetic.

**A live mini-swe-agent run makes the hints real.** Continuum's released repository already vendors mini-swe-agent with a Poisson jobs-per-second mode and attaches its IDs through `extra_body` ([Continuum swebench.py](https://github.com/Hanchenli/vllm-continuum/blob/main/mini-swe-agent/src/minisweagent/run/extra/swebench.py)), so attaching `kv_hints` the same way is a small client change, and the engine would then read keys from its own completions under the flag's default `reply` source ([memory.py:254-265](../python/sglang/srt/arg_groups/fields/memory.py)). Recording at the client's HTTP transport, as the Storage View study did for its live Open Deep Research trace, captures exact messages and wall-clock times for later replay ([Storage View](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.10178v1)). Reporting pass rate beside latency answers the divergence objection, as in Continuum; repeated runs answer stochastic decoding, as in PBKV; and lines changed per harness price the integration, as in KVTether. The sandboxes belong on separate CPU hosts, as ThunderAgent's do, because the H200 box has 16 vCPUs ([RUNBOOK.md:117](../agent_cache/RUNBOOK.md)) and Ask the Tool shows co-located load slowing tools several-fold. Batch SWE-bench agents have sub-second median gaps, so the live run will mostly exercise the GPU and host tiers; inserting human waits sampled from TraceLab at turn ends, as AKR inserts synthetic approval waits, would let it reach the SSD.

**Recording that live run and replaying it token-exactly turns it into the controlled bench.** EfficientAgent's design gives native trajectories, native reply text for the engine to parse, intervals recorded on our own serving stack, and no divergence between arms. Its known limit, intervals recorded under one arm's load, is smaller than the current one, intervals recorded on other providers' stacks.

**Baselines and names should follow the literature's vocabulary.** MORI's code is an SGLang v0.5.10 HiCache fork, the build nearest ours, though its replay client is unreleased, so our client would drive it ([MORI repo](https://github.com/cblmemo/mori)). Continuum must be named by variant. Our rivals should carry the literature's labels, `nofc_noend` as "program ID only" and `nofc` as "program ID plus release", with the hints' worth stated over them in seconds beside the rerun move, as the outside review asked ([DECISIONS.md:493-495](../agent_cache/results/campaign26/DECISIONS.md)).

**Sweeps, metrics, sensitivity and release round it out.** A concurrency sweep at fixed host memory, as MORI (20–120 programs) and ThunderAgent (24–120) run, yields the latency–load curve and the sessions per GPU under a TTFT target that our plan already lists as needed ([PAPER_PLAN.md:25](../agent_cache/PAPER_PLAN.md)); a host sweep at fixed sessions, as in EfficientAgent's 3–80 GiB per rank or MORI's GPU:CPU ratios of 1:1 to 1:4, separates memory from hints. FlatCake's delivered per-tier hit rate, SSD bandwidth and read amplification, MORI's served and recomputed fractions, and per-request hint size and engine overhead (the wire cost of our 3 KB table is unmeasured) belong beside the TTFT tables. Gap noise drawn from [t(1−s), t(1+s)] as in TokenCake, dropped hints as in SYMPHONY's 10–20% missing advisories, noisy hints as in LAMPS's 5–50% error injection, and late hints at our measured 82–94 ms delay would bound the hints' fragility. Each claim should say whether it rests on GPU runs or on the CPU room model, a line Mooncake's technical report blurs. Releasing the client, converters and configs would also set the work apart: EfficientAgent releases its replay framework, MORI does not release its client, and Marconi's artifact concedes that some of its scripts "either do not exactly reproduce the figures in the paper" or contain hand-picked numbers ([Marconi artifact](https://github.com/ruipeterpan/marconi)).

## Conclusion

The field has settled for its weakest variable, timing — load-independent sleeps, invented think times, or gaps dropped altogether — even though every tier hit rate it reports is conditional on that variable. Our design is the one most exposed to timing, because a gap distribution is its input, and that makes timing realism our largest available contribution as well as our largest risk. An evaluation that replays real-user tool and human gaps at ×1 across GPU, host and SSD, with hints a framework could actually send, would be a methodological first in this set rather than one more mechanism; the ×10 stretch currently trades that claim away. The test also cuts both ways. Long, variable human waits are where a per-key distribution should beat identity plus release, but the 100 GB result, where added memory outbid every hint, warns that the measured value can stay small, so the paper should be framed to survive either outcome: a regime map of where distributions pay, not a blanket claim that hints help.

No single workload makes both the hints and the timing real. Live SWE-bench runs make the hints, the engine's reply parsing and the coupling between tool time and load real, but they carry batch-agent timing; real-user traces carry real timing but no content, so their hint keys still come from metadata. The evaluation therefore has to be a pair — a live run that validates the interface and a real-user replay that validates the timing — with the current campaigns kept as the controlled ablation bench. That is the pairing Continuum and AKR already use, applied to the variable on which this design's claims most depend.
